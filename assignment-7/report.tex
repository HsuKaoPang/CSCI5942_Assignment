\section*{Setup}

All training and evaluation ran on one NVIDIA A40 (46\,GB) on NCSA Delta
(partition \texttt{gpuA40x4}), with the \texttt{pytorch-conda/2.12} module
(PyTorch 2.12.1, CUDA 13.0). The policy starts from the A5 full
fine-tuning run (\texttt{gpt2-medium-full}, linked as \texttt{out/sft}).
The judge is \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} with the thinking budget at zero.
Every GRPO run uses 100 steps of 8 MRs with $G = 8$, temperature 1,
at most 64 new tokens, learning rate $5 \times 10^{-6}$ and seed 1337;
only $\beta$ and the rubric change between runs.

\paragraph{What the order-swap number means for the advantage.}
The advantage is used for learning, but late in training the scores in a
group differ by only about two points ($\max - \min \approx 2$), which is
exactly how much a score can move when the order is swapped. Because of
this noise, we expect the training process to slow down.

\paragraph{Does the humans' mean leave the policy anything to gain?}
Yes: the humans' mean is 7.74 of 10, so there is room to gain, but the
policy might get a higher score than the human average not because of
better writing but because it follows the rubric's rules.

\paragraph{Eight samples at temperature 1 from \texttt{out/sft}.}
MR: \texttt{name[The Vaults], eatType[pub], priceRange[more than \pounds30],
customer rating[5 out of 5], near[Café Adriatic]}
\begin{enumerate}\small
\item The Vaults is a pub with highly promising reviews. Rated 5 out of 5 by its customers, this establishment is on the pricey side. This pub is situated near Café Adriatic.
\item The Vaults is located near Café Adriatic.  It is a high priced pub with a 5 out of 5 rating.
\item Near Café Adriatic is a high priced pub named The Vaults. this is a pub with high customer ratings. the name of the pub is House of Jeaclysm
\item There is a pub near Café Adriatic named The Vaults, it has a customer rating of 5 out of 5 and the price range is more than \pounds30.
\item The Vaults is a pub near Café Adriatic. They have a 5 out of 5 customer rating and a high price.
\item Next to Café Adriatic is a pub called The Vaults.   It cost us more than thirty euros; leaving us with a 5 out of 5 customer rating.
\item 5 out of 5 stars are typical for drinks at the Vaults pub about \pounds30 or less. It is located across from Café Adriatic.
\item The Vaults No.102a 3star pub in the higher price Range near Café Adriatic.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why a group whose scores are all equal contributes nothing.}
A group with no difference in scores gives no learning signal, because
every advantage is zero and so $-\hat A_i \log \pi_\theta = 0$.

\begin{itemize}\small

  than \pounds30.
\item \textbf{After} (\texttt{out/judge-leash}): The Vaults is a pub near

  rating of 5 out of 5.

\paragraph{What changed.}
\texttt{judge-leash} gets a higher judge score (8.75 to 9.42) and a better
rank of 8 (1.79 to 1.06), which shows that \texttt{judge-leash} gets
better results. However, BLEU does not (63.2 to 60.1). BLEU only measures
word overlap with the references, not the quality of the writing, so it
is normal that BLEU shows a difference.
Slot coverage, on the other hand, goes up (97.2\% to 99.8\%) and agrees
with the judge, because the rubric asks the description to state every
attribute in the MR, which is exactly what slot coverage measures. The
judge never sees the references, and the rubric does not ask the
description to sound like them. So the judge and the unseen metrics agree
only where the rubric asks for the same thing: the policy improves what
the rubric names and drifts away from the references on what it does not.

\paragraph{What the policy found:}
Three of the highest-scored descriptions in \texttt{samples.jsonl}; none of
the three MRs has an \texttt{eatType}:
\begin{itemize}\small
\item \texttt{judge-leash}, step 99, score 9. MR: \texttt{name[Clowns],
  priceRange[high], familyFriendly[yes], near[Café Sicilia]}\\
  ``Clowns is a child-friendly \textbf{restaurant} located near Café
  Sicilia with a high price range.''
\item \texttt{judge-noleash}, step 60, score 10. MR: \texttt{name[The
  Waterman], food[Italian], priceRange[more than \pounds30], customer
  rating[high], area[city centre], familyFriendly[yes]}\\
  ``The Waterman is an Italian \textbf{restaurant} in the city centre with
  a high customer rating, a price range of more than \pounds30 and is
  children friendly.''
\item \texttt{judge-leash}, step 90, score 9. MR: \texttt{name[The Rice
  Boat], food[Indian], priceRange[\pounds20-25], customer rating[high],
  area[city centre], familyFriendly[yes], near[Express by Holiday Inn]}\\
  ``The Rice Boat is a kid friendly Indian \textbf{restaurant} with a price
  range between \pounds20-25 in the city centre area near the Express by
  Holiday Inn with a high customer rating.''
\end{itemize}
All three add a venue type that the MR does not have. Of the 22 groups logged per run, 12 have an MR without
\texttt{eatType}; the best description added a venue type in 10 of these
12 groups for \texttt{judge-leash} and in all 12 for
\texttt{judge-noleash}, and the judge gave every one of them 9 or 10. The judge punishes contradictions (``located in All Bar
One'' for \emph{near}: 3; ``family-friendly'' for \emph{no}: 1), but not
plausible additions.

The policy also packs every attribute into one sentence with stacked
prepositions. The rubric asked for neither: this is reward hacking.

\paragraph{My rubric:}
\begin{quote}
The description states every attribute in the MR and nothing else,
never naming a venue type (restaurant, pub or coffee shop) unless the MR
has eatType, in one or two natural sentences, each with at most three
prepositions.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{What it changed:}
The venue-type rule worked: in the same 12 groups without
\texttt{eatType}, the best description added a venue type in only 1
group (10 for \texttt{judge-leash}).
The preposition rule did not (about 2.5 per longest sentence, against
2.6).

On the \texttt{given} rubric, \texttt{judge-yours} matches
\texttt{judge-leash} (9.43 against 9.42), but its BLEU returns to the SFT
level (63.1 against 60.1); under its own rubric it scores 9.07.

Two limits: the eval cannot show the venue fix, because every test MR has
an \texttt{eatType}, and the judge became noisier (13\% of scores moved on
reversal, against 8.0\%).

\section*{The Question: What Did the Judge Measure?}

The judge measured how well a description follows the rubric, not how
good it is. It does read the MR (the intruder scored lowest in 30 of 30
groups, against about 4 by chance), but 8.0\% of scores moved by 2 or
more when only the order changed. Late in training that is as large as a
group's whole spread, so part of every advantage is noise.

The judge agreed with the unseen metrics only where the rubric asks for
the same thing: slot coverage rose (97.2\% to 99.8\%), but BLEU fell (63.2
to 60.1), because the rubric never asks the text to sound like the
references. Part 4 showed what it really rewarded: a complete sentence
that does not contradict the MR, even with an invented venue type.

Each run made 800 judge calls (about 3{,}500 in total). A step took
8.0\,s: 6.3\,s sampling, 0.75\,s judging and 0.9\,s updating. A checker
would make 0 calls but save only the 0.75\,s (about 9\%); sampling is the
bottleneck.
